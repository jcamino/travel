    Before the first night

    Western Yunnan changed hands several times, and this page tells those stories one night at a time, in the places you will sleep: a Mongol army crossing the Jinsha on inflated hides in 1253, a sultan surrendering Dali in the last week of 1872, blacked-out convoys (no lights) on the Burma Road and air transports over the Hump, a guesthouse fire in Dukezong at one in the morning in 2014.

    So this is written for the hour after dinner: one chapter per hotel night, the history first and the plan second, so the practical block is always the last thing before the next chapter. The page asks at every stop whose lamp was lit here, and who put it out.

    How this page works

    The bar along the bottom of the screen always knows which night it is, where you are and how high; the row of fifteen small windows in it is the trip, past nights dimmed, tonight's lit.       I–II
      Nights I–II · Dali 大理 1,980 m

      The lake that was a capital

      The first lamp is lit beside a lake that was a capital for five hundred years before anyone in Beijing had heard of it.

      In 737 a chief called Piluoge united six small kingdoms around Erhai into one, Nanzhao, and two years later set his capital at Taihe, a few kilometres south of where you sleep tonight. The Tang sent armies to correct him: eighty thousand men in 751, broken at Xiaguan at the foot of the lake, much of the army lost to disease; a hundred thousand more in 754 under Li Mi, who never reached the water. Nanzhao ended in blood in 902 when its chief minister murdered the royal family, and the Kingdom of Dali that replaced it in 937 under Duan Siping lasted three centuries more, Buddhist enough that ten of its twenty-two kings retired into monasteries.

      1253 · The crossingAn army the chronicles put at a hundred thousand under Kublai came down through Sichuan in three columns, crossed the Jinsha on rafts of inflated hides, and took Dali in the last weeks of 1253. The Duan king surrendered, handed over his maps of Yunnan, and was kept on as Maharaja under the Mongols; Kublai, against habit, forbade the massacre of the town.

      That is the night Yunnan first belonged to an empire ruled from the north. It has belonged to one ever since, with intervals. One began in 1856,That is the night Yunnan came under an empire ruled from the north. The rebellion began in 1856, when a massacre of Muslims organised by a Qing official in Kunming set off a rising across the province. Du Wenxiu, a Muslim scholar born in Yongchang, which is Baoshan, where you will sleep on Night XI, made Dali the capital of Pingnan Guo, the Kingdom of the Pacified South, and ruled it as Sultan Suleiman for sixteen years from the walled town you walk through on your first evening.

      1872 · The sultan's last nightOn 25 December 1872 the Qing army stood at the gates. Du told his council that if he fought to the death "not even a chicken or dog will be left alive", swallowed a ball of opium, and went out to surrender in exchange for the town. He was beheaded before the opium finished its work, and the Hui of Dali were massacred anyway; one account puts the dead at ten thousand. The two Wikipedia entries date the fall of the city to late December 1872 and to January 1873; the footer says so.

      The people of the lake are the Bai, who build in white with grey-blue trim and dye in indigo at Zhoucheng. The vat and the cloth are the colours of this page: the ground is the dye, the text is the undyed cotton. On Night II the Mid-Autumn moon rises over Erhai all but full, some twenty-eight hours short of it; it is exactly full at 00:49 on the 27th, the night Javier lands. The night before that is Icíar's birthday, which is the best possible reason to be beside this lake.

      III–VI
      Nights III–VI · Lijiang and Shuhe 丽江 2,400 m

      The rafts at Stone Drum

      Lijiang's fortune was made by deciding which side to be on when a Mongol army arrived at the water.

      "When the army crossed the Jinsha River, the Khan accepted the surrender and the welcome of the leader of the Naxi tribe." That is all the chronicle says, and it was enough. Local tradition places the crossing at Stone Drum, the first great bend of the Yangtze, which you pass on the road to the gorge on Day 7; the drum itself is a Ming marble tablet from the 1550s celebrating a Naxi victory over a Tibetan army, put up by the same family that had chosen well three centuries earlier. The Yuan made Lijiang a prefecture in 1278. The Ming confirmed the Mu family as hereditary chieftains, and for four hundred years the Mu collected the taxes of the road in silver and grain and sent them on to the court.

      Dongba, the script of the Naxi priests, is the only pictographic writing system in the world still actively maintained, about a thousand glyphs and a number that is still fluid because new ones are coined. Its great foreign reader was Joseph Rock, Vienna-born, an American citizen from 1913, who settled in the village of Nguluko, now Yuhu, at the foot of Jade Dragon in about 1922 and kept it as his base for twenty-seven years, filing plant specimens to Harvard and long illustrated dispatches to National Geographic. He left for the last time in August 1949, ahead of the People's Liberation Army. Hilton never said in public that Rock's articles gave him Shangri-La; the actress Jane Wyatt recalled him saying so in private, and the Wikipedia entry calls the theory disputed. The town on Night VII was renamed on that basis.

      1996 · 19:14, 3 FebruaryMagnitude 6.6, up to 322 dead, more than 17,000 injured, some 358,000 buildings down. The reconstruction, paid for by the province and the World Bank, pulled down the concrete blocks and put back traditional streets, bridges and canals, and that work is what carried Lijiang onto the World Heritage list on 4 December 1997, with Baisha and Shuhe listed alongside it.

      After the listing, rents rose. 90 to 95 per cent of the old town's residents moved out because they could let their houses to shopkeepers for more than they had ever earned in them; over nine-tenths of the housing is now guesthouses and shops. So you sleep in Shuhe, the smaller caravan village a few kilometres north that was on the same list and has kept more of its people.

      IX–X
      Nights IX–X · Shaxi 沙溪 2,100 m

      Where the muleteers slept

      Armies moved the frontier. For a thousand years trade held it in place, tea going one way and horses coming the other.

      The Tea Horse Road is the name for the caravan routes that carried brick tea out of Yunnan and Sichuan into Tibet and brought Tibetan ponies back, a trade the Song court ran as policy, since a state that grows tea can buy cavalry from a country that cannot. The Yunnan branch went up through Dali, Lijiang and Gyalthang, exactly the way you have just come, and Shaxi was a trading post on it from the Tang, at its richest under the Ming and the Qing. Porters carried sixty to ninety kilos of tea on their backs, more than their own weight. The muleteers slept in the caravanserai inns around Sideng with their loads beside the bed, and on the eve of a market the stage in the square was lit for opera.

      2001 · The listingThe Sideng market square went on the World Monuments Watch list of the hundred most endangered sites in 2001, and the restoration that followed was a cooperation between ETH Zurich, the Swiss federal technical university, and the county government of Jianchuan. It is why this is the one intact caravan town left on the road: the stage, the Xingjiao temple of the Bai (Ming), the gate, the lanes, and nothing dressed up.

      What is left of the road is the Friday market. Once a week the valley comes down to Sideng, Bai and Yi farmers, and for a few hours the square is a market again. You arrive on a Friday. Leave the plateau early and you catch the end of it.

      XI
      Night XI · Baoshan 保山 1,650 m

      The Burma Road

      One night to break the journey. This chapter is mostly about a road.

      The Burma Road was cut through these hills in 1937 and 1938 by two hundred thousand Burmese and Chinese labourers, most of them with hand tools: 717 miles from Lashio in Burma up through Baoshan and Dali to Kunming, the only overland supply line left to a China whose coast Japan had closed. Your car tomorrow runs on its successor for most of the way to Tengchong. The convoys ran blacked out, and the road climbed and dropped through the Salween gorge on switchbacks that killed more drivers than the bombers did.

      1942 · 4 MayJapan took Lashio on 29 April 1942 and the road was cut at its far end. On 4 May Japanese aircraft bombed Baoshan, a town crammed with refugees off the road from Burma; a figure of ten thousand dead in the raid is the one usually quoted. The next day, 5 May, Chinese engineers blew the Huitong bridge over the Salween to stop the Japanese column, and the river became the front line for two years. Chinese state media also say Japanese planes dropped cholera over Baoshan that week and put the epidemic's toll at 210,000; the raid and the epidemic numbers are contested and this page does not rely on either. The footer says which sources disagree.

      Du Wenxiu, the sultan of Night II, was born here when it was called Yongchang. On 4 February 1945 the first convoy from India in nearly three years came up through these towns to Kunming, under banners the villagers had painted in two languages.

      XII–XIV
      Nights XII–XIV · Tengchong and Heshun 腾冲 1,640 m

      Fifty kilometres from Myanmar

      Three nights fifty kilometres from a border, on the side of it where the lights are on.

      With the road cut, the Americans flew. From April 1942 until the summer of 1945 the India–China airlift carried about 650,000 tons over the eastern Himalaya from Assam to Kunming, in C-47s and then in the C-46, the biggest twin-engined aircraft yet built and a temperamental one. It cost between five and six hundred aircraft, and 1,659 crew dead or missing. From the autumn of 1943, under Colonel Thomas Hardin, the Hump was flown at night as well as by day and no scheduled flight was cancelled for weather; when General Tunner took over from him in September 1944 he made it round the clock, and in July 1945, the last full month, 662 aircraft carried 71,042 tons over the mountains.

      1944 · 11 May to 14 SeptemberOn the ground, the Chinese Expeditionary Force crossed the Salween on 11 May 1944 and laid siege to Tengchong, which the Japanese had held for two years behind its Ming wall. The last positions inside the wall fell on 14 September (one Wikipedia list gives the 13th). Nearly ten thousand Chinese soldiers died to take it, and the town was destroyed in the taking. Their cemetery, Guoshang, was finished on 7 July 1945: 3,346 small stones in rows up the hill of Xiaotuanpo.

      Before the war, jade crossed this border. Burmese jadeite came over the passes to Tengchong, the first place in China where it was cut and sold, and the families of Heshun, the village next door, went the other way, to Mandalay and Rangoon, and sent money home. What they built with it was a farming village of stone lanes and courtyard houses and, in 1928, a library, expanded from a reading society the emigrants had founded in 1924 and still the largest rural library in China, with about eighty thousand volumes and the odd Western cornice on a Chinese hall. Javier leaves from here at midday on Day 13: Heshun in the morning, then the hilltop airport.

      Fire undergroundTengchong sits on a field of sixty-eight volcanic cones raised where India pushes into Asia, a collision that began about 59 million years ago and is still going; the last big eruption was around 5750 BCE. Rehai, the "hot sea", is the largest geothermal field of the lot, and Dagunguo, the big boiling pot, is its showpiece. Day 14 is for it.

      On 1 February 2021 Myanmar's army overthrew the elected government; by the end of March people were leaving the protests for the border hills to train with the ethnic armies, and the Kachin Independence Army was already taking bases near Laiza, on the Yunnan frontier. In October 2023 an alliance of those armies launched Operation 1027 and took Laukkai, the capital of the Kokang zone that abuts Yunnan further south. By November 2025 OCHA counted nearly 3.6 million people displaced inside the country, the most on record. Through all of it China has kept building the China–Myanmar Economic Corridor, a Belt and Road spur from Kunming through Muse and Mandalay to a deep-water port at Kyaukphyu on the Bay of Bengal: oil and gas pipelines finished in 2013 and 2017, and a 431-kilometre Muse–Mandalay railway costed at nine billion dollars, through country that is at war.

      Keeping this on your phone

      • On an iPhone: open this page in Safari, tap the Share button at the bottom, scroll down and tap Add to Home Screen. It then opens like an app, and works with no signal at all.
      • On Android: open the menu in Chrome and tap Install app or Add to Home screen.
      • Everything, photographs included, is already inside this one page. Once it has loaded once, it never needs the internet again.

      The forecast and the night log, offline

      • Every night card shows the weather in one of three ways. Until the forecast opens (sixteen days ahead: 9 September for the first night, 23 September for the last day) it gives the usual climate for that stop and those dates, from the station normals, worded as such. Once open, and whenever the phone is online, the forecast comes from Open-Meteo: the night's low first, then the day's high, the sky in words, and the chance of rain when it is 30% or more, with the time it was fetched. Offline, the last forecast fetched is shown unchanged with that time.
      • Sunset and moonrise under each date are worked out on the phone from the stop's latitude and longitude, in China Standard Time, to within a few minutes; the small moon is that night's phase.
      • The night log is one line per night, saved on this phone only. Postcard makes a dark postcard of the line with the chapter's window and shares it as a picture; nothing is sent anywhere on its own.
      • The lamp control in the night strip (bed, room, noon) raises the text towards white for daylight reading. The page never turns light; the Day 15 card, lit in Shanghai's neon because that night is spent there, is the one exception.

      Nights I–II · Dali

      • Nanzhao, Wikipedia: Piluoge and 737, the capital at Taihe in 739, the Tang defeats of 751 (80,000) and 754 (100,000, Li Mi), the end in 902.
      • Kingdom of Dali, Wikipedia: Duan Siping in 937, ten of twenty-two kings retiring as monks, the three columns converging on Dali in late 1253, Duan Xingzhi's surrender and the Maharaja title.
      • Du Yuting and Chen Lufan, "Did Kublai Khan's conquest of the Dali Kingdom give rise to the mass migration of the Thai people?", Journal of the Siam Society 77 (1989): the army of a hundred thousand, the leather rafts and three routes across the Jinsha, the Naxi leader's welcome, the edict against slaughter.
      • Kublai Khan, Wikipedia: the three-column campaign, the sparing of Dali's residents, Uriyangkhadai's pacification of Yunnan by 1256. Kublai was born in 1215, so 38 in 1253.
      • Stone Drum and the First Bend of the Yangtze, TravelChinaGuide: the local tradition of the 1253 crossing at Shigu and the Ming marble drum commemorating a Naxi victory over a Tibetan army. This is tradition, not a chronicle, and the page says so.
      • Erhai, Wikipedia: 1,972 m, forty kilometres north to south and seven or eight across, 250 km², Shangguan at the head and Xiaguan at the foot, the Cangshan to the west. Used for the Erhai sketch.
      • Panthay Rebellion, Wikipedia: 1856, the Kunming massacre and Shuxing'a, Pingnan Guo, the fall of Dali given as January 1873, the opium and the beheading, about a million dead in the rebellion as a whole.
      • Du Wenxiu, Wikipedia: born 1823 in Yongchang (Baoshan), the xiucai degree at sixteen, the council on 25 December 1872 and the "chicken or dog" line, death on 26 December 1872, the massacre of ten thousand Hui, the seal's two faces. Disagreement: this entry has Dali falling in the last days of December 1872, the rebellion entry says January 1873; the page gives both.
      • 国务院办公厅关于2026年部分节假日安排的通知, People's Daily, 4 November 2025: Mid-Autumn holiday 25 to 27 September 2026, National Day 1 to 7 October, working days on 20 September and 10 October.
      • US Naval Observatory, moon phases 2026: new moon 11 September 03:27 UT, first quarter 18 September 20:44, full moon 26 September 16:49 UT, which is 00:49 on 27 September in China, last quarter 3 October 13:25, new moon 10 October 15:50. The cover moon and the small moon on each card are interpolated between these instants, and "twenty-eight hours short" on the evening of 25 September is counted from them.

      Nights III–VI · Lijiang and Shuhe

      • Nakhi people, Wikipedia: Lijiang Prefecture established by the Yuan in 1278, the Mu chieftains under the Ming and their taxes in silver and grain, Dongba and its Bön roots, the 2020 population.
      • Dongba symbols, Wikipedia: "the only pictographic writing system in the world still actively maintained", about a thousand glyphs.
      • Joseph Rock, Wikipedia: born Vienna 1884, naturalised 1913, Nguluko as his base for twenty-seven years from about 1922, left China for the last time in August 1949.
      • Shangri-La, Wikipedia: Rock's National Geographic articles of the late 1920s and early 1930s as the possible source of Hilton's setting, described as disputed; Zhongdian renamed on 17 December 2001.
      • "Journey to Shangri-La", National Geographic Traveler, which claims Hilton found his inspiration in Rock "by his own admission". The Jane Wyatt recollection is in "Shangri-La: A Case of Myth-taken Identity", Epoch Magazine, which also notes that Hilton never said so in public. The page keeps this at "usual suspect".
      • 1996 Lijiang earthquake, Wikipedia: 19:14 on 3 February 1996, Mw 6.6, up to 322 dead, more than 17,000 injured, 358,000 buildings destroyed, and the World Bank reconstruction that led to the listing.
      • Old Town of Lijiang, Wikipedia: inscribed 4 December 1997 with Baisha and Shuhe; 90 to 95 per cent of residents moved out; over 90 per cent of residential land now commercial.
      • Tiger Leaping Gorge, Wikipedia: about 3,790 m from river to peak, 15 km long, Jade Dragon 5,596 m and Haba 5,396 m, the 25-metre narrows and the tiger.
      • Jade Dragon's single ascent, 8 May 1987, is from the current page's own research notes and its caption; the mountain has been closed to climbers since.

      Nights VII–VIII · Shangri-La

      • Shangri-La City, Wikipedia: Zhongdian and Gyalthang ("royal plains"), the renaming on 17 December 2001, the fire "in the early morning of 11 January 2014", 242 homes and shops destroyed and 2,600 displaced, about half the old town, the 3,160 m elevation.
      • "Shangri-La is no more", The World (PRX), 13 January 2014: the fire started around one in the morning and lasted ten hours; about 250 houses and two-thirds of the old town by their count.
      • Associated Press via Fox News: the detention of the Ruyi Inn's operator, the heater left on and the curtain, nearly ten hours, 343 houses. Disagreement: 242 buildings (Wikipedia), about 250 (PRX) and 343 houses (AP); the page gives the first and the last. The empty hydrants are reported in the Chinese press summaries of the same days.
      • Lost Horizon, Wikipedia: 1933, James Hilton, the Zhongdian renaming.
      • Ganden Sumtseling Monastery, Wikipedia: founded 1679 by the Fifth Dalai Lama, Gelug, "Little Potala", damaged in the Cultural Revolution and rebuilt from 1983, about 700 monks in 200 residences, 3,380 m, 5 km from the town.
      • Tibet travel regulations, The Land of Snows, and Tibet permit guide: TAR versus other Tibetan areas: the Tibet Travel Permit applies to the Tibet Autonomous Region only; Deqen/Diqing in Yunnan needs no permit.
      • "China sees 888 million domestic trips during eight-day holiday", gov.cn, 9 October 2025, from the Ministry of Culture and Tourism.

      Nights IX–X · Shaxi

      • Tea Horse Road, Wikipedia: tea for Tibetan ponies from at least the Song, the route through Dali, Lijiang and Shangri-La, porters carrying 60 to 90 kg.
      • Shaxi, Yunnan, Wikipedia: a trading point from the Tang, prosperity under the Ming and Qing, Sideng on the World Monuments Watch list of 100 most endangered sites in 2001, the ETH Zurich and Jianchuan County cooperation, the Ming Xingjiao temple, Bai and Yi.
      • The Friday market hours (about 10:00 into early afternoon) are the trip's own notes, carried over unchanged.

      Night XI · Baoshan

      • Burma Road, Wikipedia: built 1937 to 1938 by 200,000 labourers, 717 miles from Lashio through Baoshan and Dali to Kunming, Lashio taken 29 April 1942, the Huitong bridge demolished 5 May 1942, the first convoy by the Ledo route reaching Kunming on 4 February 1945.
      • "Horrors of Japanese WWII biowarfare", China Daily Hong Kong: the raid of 4 May 1942 (54 aircraft), "some 10,000" killed in it, and the claim of cholera bombs and 210,000 epidemic deaths. Contested: the ten thousand is a round figure repeated across Chinese sources; the cholera toll is a state-media claim and this page does not adopt either number as fact.
      • Battle of Northern Burma and Western Yunnan, Wikipedia: the Salween as the line from 1942, the crossing plan of April 1944, the Tengchong, Mount Song and Longling battles.

      Nights XII–XIV · Tengchong

      • The Hump, Wikipedia: April 1942 to August 1945 with the last flights in November 1945, about 650,000 tons, 594 aircraft lost, 1,659 personnel killed or missing, the first C-46s in April 1943, Hardin introducing night missions and refusing to cancel flights for weather, Tunner in command from 4 September 1944, and July 1945 as the peak month: 662 aircraft, 71,042 tons.
      • "Over the Hump to China", Air & Space Forces Magazine: Colonel Thomas O. Hardin in command of the airlift from September 1943 and beginning night flights, monthly deliveries up from 4,624 to 23,675 tons under him, Tunner taking over in September 1944, 509 aircraft lost, 1,314 dead and 345 missing, the C-46 as the largest twin-engined aircraft built to that point. Disagreement: 594 versus 509 aircraft; the page says "between five and six hundred".
      • "This day in 1944: the Chinese capture Tengchong", Daniel Jackson: the capture on 14 September 1944 after fifty-one days of siege and house-to-house fighting. The Wikipedia campaign list gives the battle as 11 May to 13 September; the page gives both dates.
      • "World War II cemetery in Yunnan receives national recognition", GoKunming, and The Graveyard of the National Heroes, China Underground: 3,346 tombs, construction from 1944 and completion on 7 July 1945, nearly 10,000 Chinese dead in retaking the town, the 20th Group Army and Xiaotuanpo.
      • Heshun guide, China Underground, and "Heshun library, the earliest and largest village library in China", China.org.cn, 2009: the reading club founded in 1924 by Heshun emigrants in Burma, expanded into the library in 1928, the largest village library in China with about 80,000 volumes, the emigrants in Burma, Tengchong as the first distribution centre for Burmese jade and amber in China.
      • Tengchong, Wikipedia: Heshun 4 km west of the centre, the airport at Tuofeng 12 km out, 151 km of border with Myanmar, Houqiao among its towns; and Gaoligong Mountains, Wikipedia: along the west bank of the Nujiang (Salween), the divide between the Salween and the Irrawaddy, main peak 5,128 m. Used for the section and the border sketch.
      • Battle of Mount Song, Wikipedia: 4 June to 7 September 1944, the mountain blocking the road immediately behind the Salween. Used for the Burma Road sketch.
      • Tengchong volcanic field, Wikipedia: 68 pyroclastic cones, the last confirmed large eruption around 5750 BCE, the India–Asia collision from about 59 million years ago, Rehai as the largest and most active geothermal field.
      • Myanmar civil war (2021–present), Wikipedia: the coup of 1 February 2021, protesters enlisting with border insurgents from late March, the KIA taking Alaw Bum near Laiza on 25 March 2021, Operation 1027 and Laukkai in October 2023. Stale there: its 1.6 million displaced is OCHA's figure from March 2023; the page uses the current one below.
      • Myanmar Humanitarian Update No. 50, OCHA, 17 November 2025: nearly 3.6 million people internally displaced nationwide, the highest on record, with new displacement from the fighting of October and November.
      • China–Myanmar Economic Corridor, Wikipedia: Yunnan through Muse and Mandalay to Kyaukphyu, the 431 km Muse–Mandalay railway at about US$9 billion, the pipelines of 2013 and 2017, a Belt and Road corridor.
      • Black Marble 2016, NASA Earth Observatory, on Wikimedia Commons: the night composite cropped for the last window; the border line and place names were drawn on for this page from their coordinates and are approximate.

      The sky and the weather

      • Sunset and moonrise on each card are computed on the phone: the sun from the NOAA solar-position equations, the moon from the Astronomical Almanac's low-precision series, in China Standard Time. Checked for six of the nights against the US Naval Observatory's rise and set tables, which agreed to the minute.
      • The climate stand-ins are the 1991–2020 station normals in the Wikipedia climate tables for Dali, Lijiang, Shangri-La, Jianchuan (for Shaxi), Baoshan and Tengchong: mean daily high and low for September and October, rounded. Tengchong's is the wettest station of the six in both months.
